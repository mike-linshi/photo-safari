# Design Notes — Pressure-Testing "Photo Safari"

This is the record of the debate: what the original pitch got right, what would
have sunk it with award juries, and why the final game is shaped the way it is.

## What the original pitch got right

1. **The core fantasy is gold.** "Wildlife photographer on safari" is warm,
   universal, non-violent, and — remarkably — nearly untouched in modern board
   games. Juries reward fresh themes tightly married to mechanics.
2. **Limited exposures is the whole game.** A disposable camera is a built-in
   press-your-luck engine: every shot spent is a shot you can't take later.
   This is the *decision* the game is about, and it's a great one.
3. **A living, revealing board.** Flip-to-discover (Carcassonne/Seafarers DNA)
   plus animals that move creates a board state that is never the same twice
   and generates stories ("the rain pushed the elephants right into my frame").

## What had to change, and why

### 1. Roll-and-move → speed choice (the biggest cut)
Dice movement (Monopoly, Life) is the single most-cited flaw in dated designs:
it converts your one decision per turn into a coin flip. Award-calibre design
puts **luck in the world, not in your hands**. So: you always choose —
**Careful (2 tiles)** or **Fast (4 tiles, but you spook nearby animals)**.
Movement is reliable; the *world* is uncertain. The spook rule also converts
speed into a thematic trade-off: rushing at wildlife scatters it. That's a rule
that teaches itself.

### 2. 20×20 (400 tiles) → 9×9 (81 tiles)
Four hundred tiles is a production non-starter (Spiel des Jahres winners are
famously lean — Kingdomino ships 48 dominoes) and a 20-minute setup for a
60-minute game. A 9×9 grid keeps the "vast unknown" feeling because *you can
still only see one tile around your jeep* — the fog does the work, not the
acreage. Density, not size, creates discovery.

### 3. 20 exposures → 8
With 20 shots over a ~10-round game, you'd photograph everything and decide
nothing. Scarcity is where the tension lives: 8 shots, 10 rounds, and Golden
Hour twice a day means you will *feel* every press of the shutter. (Playtest
knob: if photos feel too precious, try 10; if spammy, 6.)

### 4. "What's in the picture?" needed a physical answer
The pitch scored "what's in your photo" without defining a photo. The answer
became the game's signature component: a **3×3 viewfinder frame** you lay on
the board, centered on your jeep. It's tactile, it demos in one second, it
photographs beautifully on a convention table — every award winner has one
iconic component (Camel Up's pyramid, Azul's tiles, Wingspan's birdfeeder).
And it makes *positioning* the skill: composition is literally geometry.

### 5. Added a clock with texture: the day track & Golden Hour
The pitch had rounds but no arc. Real wildlife photography is ruled by light,
so the game is too: 10 rounds = Dawn→Dusk twice, and photos at Dawn/Dusk score
+4. Now *when* to shoot matters as much as *where*, the endgame telegraphs
itself naturally ("last Golden Hour, everyone's holding two exposures…"), and
the theme does the teaching again.

### 6. Added secret assignments (hidden goals)
Pure shared scoring makes players converge on the same herd and turns the game
into a parking lot. Two secret magazine assignments per player ("Walking with
Giants: elephant + giraffe in one photo") pull players toward *different*
corners of the savanna, create the classic hidden-information "why is she
camped by the marsh?" tension, and add an endgame reveal moment.

### 7. Animal behaviour: a system, not a simulation
The pitch wanted moving animals and weather but no mechanic. The trap is
bookkeeping (moving 30 standees by rule every round kills pacing). Solution:
animals move **only when nature says so** — one event card per round (rain
pulls herds to water, predators hunt, migrations arrive) plus the spook rule.
Each event moves many animals with one simple instruction, and each one
creates or destroys photo opportunities, which is exactly what weather does to
real photographers.

### What was considered and rejected

- **Kill shots / poachers** — memorable but tonally wrong for the family
  audience that wins the big awards. The Hunt event keeps predator drama
  without blood on the table.
- **Per-animal movement dice** — thematic, glacial. Cut (kept as a variant).
- **Photo cards you collect** — fights the viewfinder; scoring the live board
  through a frame is more novel than drafting cards.
- **Simultaneous turns** — faster but wrecks the spook/positioning interplay.

## The award thesis

A jury sees: a one-sentence pitch ("drive, frame, click — but you only have 8
shots"), a signature component (the viewfinder), zero player elimination, no
direct conflict but constant *positional* interaction (animals you spook flee
toward or away from rivals; the herd only gathers once), luck players can plan
around, rules that fit on a postcard, and a story generator — every game ends
with players showing each other their photo logs. That's the Spiel des Jahres
shape. The depth for enthusiasts lives in exposure economics and reading the
Nature deck's probabilities.

## Playtest agenda (the honest unknowns)

1. **Point tuning** — are herds (+4) too strong vs. rare singles (rhino 8)?
   Track winning strategies; both should be viable.
2. **Exposure count** — 8 is the theory; watch for players finishing with 3+
   unused (too many) or shooting everything before Day 2 (too few).
3. **Spook radius** — adjacent-only may be too forgiving at Fast speed; the
   alternative is "every tile you passed."
4. **Multiplayer solitaire risk** — if players ignore each other, strengthen
   interaction: e.g., an animal photographed 'startles' and moves 1, or first-
   to-photograph-a-species bonuses.
5. **Analysis paralysis at Golden Hour** — if final rounds drag, add a sand
   timer "the light is fading" gimmick (on-theme pressure).
